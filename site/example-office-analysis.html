Worked example: one office, one year

A year of half-hourly data from a small office, run through the analyser. The office and its data are made up, but every figure below is what the analyser reports for it.

Last updated 14 November 2026

A third of this office's electricity (33%) was used while the building was shut. After allowing for what never switches off, that is about £2,800 a year at 25p/kWh, and almost half of it happens on Saturdays.

Made-up data. The office is invented so you can see the method without anyone's real data. Download the file and load it into the analyser to follow along.

The headline figures

Electricity a year79,191 kWhScaled from 363 days of valid readings (99.5% coverage)
Energy cost a year£19,798At a flat 25p/kWh, before standing and capacity charges

Where the energy goes

The analyser splits every half hour at the base load. The three parts add up to the total:

  • Base load, 29% (22,606 kWh): what runs around the clock.
  • Open hours above base load, 57% (45,062 kWh): the building doing its job.
  • Closed-hours excess, 14% (11,035 kWh): use above base load while the building should be shut. This is where to look first.

What the analyser found

1. Out-of-hours use: £2,776 a year (high impact)

The breakdown by day type is the useful part:

WhenAbove base load
Saturdays5,155 kWh
Weekday evenings and nights3,750 kWh
Bank holidays1,408 kWh
Sundays722 kWh

Saturdays account for almost half. Sundays are quiet, so this isn't plant running all weekend: the building is being used on some Saturdays. Either that's planned (then the calendar should say so, and the figure falls) or a Saturday booking is running the whole building.

2. Seven bank holidays look like working days (medium impact)

Seven bank holidays used at least 80% of a normal weekday, including Christmas Day and Boxing Day. If the office was shut, heating and ventilation schedules probably don't know about bank holidays. If it was open, the calendar should be changed.

3. Early starts and late finishes: £629 a year (low impact)

Demand rises before opening and stays up after closing: about 1,250 kWh a year above base load in the two hours before opening, and the same in the two hours after closing. Optimum-start and time-clock settings are the usual cause.

Supply capacity

Assuming an agreed supply capacity of 40 kVA and a power factor of 0.95, the highest half hour reaches 27 kVA, 67% of capacity. Keeping a 10% safety margin, the site could take about 9 kW of constant new load. That's enough for modest EV charging, not a heat pump for the whole building. See supply capacity and kVA.

What we'd do next

  1. Confirm the opening hours, including Saturdays. Every out-of-hours figure depends on them.
  2. Check the heating and ventilation schedules for bank holidays and start times.
  3. Walk the building on a Saturday to see what's on and who is using it.
  4. Look at the base load: 2.6 kW running all year is about 22,800 kWh, roughly £5,700 at 25p/kWh. See how to bring it down.

57% of this office's electricity is used while it's open, during the working day, which is when on-site solar generates. The UK Solar Calculator estimates what a roof system would save.

Questions people ask

Is this real data?

No. The office and its readings are made up, so the method can be shown without anyone's real data. Every figure is what the analyser reports for the example file.

How long does an analysis like this take?

Loading a year of data takes about a minute. Confirming the opening hours and reading the findings takes longer, and matters more.

Are these figures savings?

No. They are what each pattern costs now at 25p/kWh. A saving depends on what you change.
